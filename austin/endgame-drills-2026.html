Root-pattern instances
Root cause — one habit, three symptoms: When Austin has a winning position (+3.5 or more), he plays moves that look correct by general principles (active piece, central knight, pawn advance) instead of first scanning for forcing moves (checks, captures, threats). The engine consistently recommends a specific forcing line; Austin plays the "reasonable" alternative.

The fix is one rule: Before every move in a winning position, ask "What checks can I deliver right now?" — before considering any positional plan. This is called CCT (Checks · Captures · Threats) discipline, and the JSON shows Austin is skipping it in over half his losses and draws from winning positions.

The 4 Conditions for Bxh7+ to Work (memorise)
  1. Bishop on the a2–g8 diagonal (c2, d3, b3, e4) — pointing at h7.
  2. Queen available for Qh5 (or Qd3) — will follow Bxh7+ Kxh7 with Qh5+.
  3. Knight reachable on g5 — after Qh5+ Kg8, Ng5 maintains the attack.
  4. No g6 pawn defending h7 — if g6 is occupied, the king escapes to g7 safely.
When all 4 are true: play Bxh7+ immediately. Do not "set it up" — the defender can break the conditions in one move.

Forcing Check Checklist (before any piece move in a +3 or better position)
  1. List every available check. Ask: "Can my queen check anywhere right now? Can my rook?" If yes — evaluate that check before choosing any other move.
  2. Ask: does this check win material or threaten mate? Checks that attack a piece or threaten the back rank almost always deserve to be played.
  3. Passive moves when you're winning are dangerous. Rb8, Re2, Ra1 — all look "solid" but hand the initiative back. The initiative is worth more than the rook square.

Austin played Re8 (rook to 8th rank — looks attacking) when the correct move was bxa3 — a quiet pawn push that passed a pawn immediately. Two moves later: Kh8? (best = Bxe5, cpl = 548), again choosing a king-safety move over a material-winning capture.
Won Position Decision Tree
  1. Check first: "What forcing moves exist?" Checks → Captures → Threats. Only after listing all three do you consider positional plans.
  2. The best move is often not the "most active" move. Rb6 is less glamorous than Bh5. Nf5 is less classical than Nf3. But they attack something specific — which matters most when the position is already won.
  3. Pawn passes beat piece improvements. bxa3 (passing a pawn) beats Re8 (developing a rook). A new passer is an irreversible threat; a rook on e8 can be driven off.
  4. Don't "improve" pieces when you can win material. Centralizing a piece follows general principles. Taking free material or creating an unstoppable passer follows the position's specific logic.

Endgame Principles (these four cover all four positions above)
  1. King safety before pawn push — Kh2 before f5, always.
  2. Activate the passer, not the rook — push d3 to force White to react; don't park the rook on d5 to defend.
  3. Active rook on 5th rank beats defensive rook on 4th — Rb5 over Ra4 in any similar structure.
  4. In a pawn race, push the pawn; don't escort it — the king often gets in the way.

The One Rule to Apply at the Board (commit this)

Whenever Austin has a clearly winning position (+3 or more) or is about to play any piece move, pause and ask three questions in this exact order:

  1. Checks: What checks can I deliver right now? (Any of my pieces landing on a square that attacks the king?)
  2. Captures: Can I take any hanging or undefended piece?
  3. Threats: What is my most concrete threat — not "improve position" but something specific Black must answer?

Only after answering all three does Austin choose his move. If the answer to #1 is "yes, I can deliver Qd4+" — that check must be evaluated before any rook move to e2 or a1. If the answer is "yes, Bxh7+ is available" — it must be evaluated before any queen centralization. This single habit would have prevented at least 10 of the 19 identified critical moments.
