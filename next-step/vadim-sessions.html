SESSION 1 Taimanov Cardinal Rules — 6.Ndb5 → d6! and 6.Be3 → Bb4!

Why this session exists: Austin blundered in a rated game (PAM R7) by playing 6...Bc5?? instead of 6...d6! — the cardinal error that costs the game immediately. At NAYCC, Fernando (2085), Pranav (1922), Zhuoyuan (1890), and Emerson (1909) all open 1.e4. The Taimanov tabiya will arise in multiple games. Austin must respond to 6.Ndb5 with d6! and to 6.Be3 with Bb4! in under 2 seconds each. The goal of this session is not explanation — it's repetition until the response is automatic.

The Taimanov Tabiya

Every drill in this session starts from this position:

1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 e6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nc6 5.Nc3 Nf6 ← Taimanov Sicilian. White must now choose a 6th move.

Drill A — 6.Ndb5: The Cardinal Rule

RULE: 6.Ndb5 → play 6...d6! in under 2 seconds. Zero thinking required.
This is not a calculation. It is a reflex. The move d6! blocks the Nd6+ fork forever. Nothing else matters on this move.

Why 6.Ndb5 is threatening

The Ndb5 aims for Nd6+ — a knight fork winning the rook on a8 (if the king hasn't castled) or creating a permanent dominant outpost. White also threatens Nxe6! forkng the queen and bishop if Black is careless. Only 6...d6! permanently stops both ideas.

Why NOT 6...Bc5?? — The PAM R7 Blunder

6...Bc5?? loses immediately. After 7.Bf4! O-O 8.Bc7! — the bishop lands on c7 attacking the queen on d8. The queen must move, and White then plays 9.Nd6! with a dominant knight that cannot be removed. The position is fundamentally broken. This is exactly what happened in PAM R7.

Main line after 6...d6! — the queenside roller

6.Ndb5 d6! ← INSTANT. Clock cost: 0 seconds. 7.Bf4 e5 8.Bg5 a6 ← kick the Nb5 9.Na3 b5 ← Na3 is stuck on the rim — "a knight on the rim is dim" 10... b4! ← kick it further. Na3 is permanently offside. 11... Bb7 ← complete the bishop battery 12... Rc8 ← rook on open file, queenside pressure Plan: ...Nd4 → ...Nxe2+ or ...Nc2+ fork ideas. White's Na3 never returns to the game.

If White plays 7.N1c3 (instead of 7.Bf4)

6.Ndb5 d6! 7.N1c3 a6 8.Na3 b5 ← same queenside expansion. The Na3 ends up on the rim regardless. 9... Bb7 ...Rc8 ← standard queenside roller
Key insight: In every 6.Ndb5 line, White's knight ends up on a3 after ...a6. A knight on a3 is the worst piece in chess — it takes 3+ moves to reactivate. Austin's queenside expansion wins the battle of tempi every time.

Vadim Drill A — 15 minutes

  • Set up the tabiya (FEN above). Vadim plays 6.Ndb5. Austin plays 6...d6! — measure response time. Goal: under 2 seconds.
  • Repeat 6.Ndb5 → 6...d6 ten times in a row. No discussion between repetitions. Build reflex.
  • Play through main line: 7.Bf4 e5 8.Bg5 a6 9.Na3 b5 10...b4! — Austin narrates the plan ("Na3 is stuck, I'm pushing queenside").
  • Vadim plays 7.N1c3 as a variation. Austin plays ...a6 ...b5 → same structure.
  • Test: Vadim plays the illegal attempt 6...Bc5 (shows Austin what NOT to play), then shows the 7.Bf4 Bc7 refutation briefly.

Drill B — 6.Be3: The Pin Response

RULE: 6.Be3 → play 6...Bb4! immediately. Pin the Nc3.
The Nc3 is the key defensive piece — it supports the Nd4 AND controls d5. Pinning it with Bb4 creates instant problems for White. This is the same idea as Austin's Nimzo-Indian: pin the knight on c3 before it takes over.

Why 6...Bb4! is the best move

The Be3 is move 1 of the English Attack (Be3 → f3 → Qd2 → O-O-O → g4). Playing Bb4! immediately disrupts this plan because:

  • White can no longer play 7.f3 freely — the Nc3 is pinned to the king
  • If White expands with 7.Ndb5 (trying the Ndb5 idea), Austin plays 7...d6 and can respond to a3 with Bxc3+! breaking White's structure
  • 86% of master games in the Taimanov recommend Bb4! at this point (from Austin's opening-novelty-trees analysis)

Branch A — 7.Ndb5 (most common)

6.Be3 Bb4! 7.Ndb5 d6 8.a3? Bxc3+! ← take immediately. Knight on c3 becomes awkward. 9.Nxc3 d5! ← central break WHILE White's knight is blocked by bxc3 structure 10.exd5 Nxd5 11.Nxd5 Qxd5 ← Black has active queen + open center. Equal or slightly better.
The timing principle: After Bxc3+ and bxc3, White's c-pawns are doubled. The c3 pawn blocks the Nb5's retreat. This is exactly when ...d5! is most powerful — White cannot recapture smoothly.

Branch B — 7.f3 (full English Attack)

6.Be3 Bb4! 7.f3 Nxd4! ← simplify immediately — don't let White build the attack 8.Bxd4 Bxc3+! ← same exchange, get the bishop pair 9.bxc3 ← White has doubled c-pawns. Austin has bishop pair. 9... e5 ← kick the Be4 diagonal, claim space 10.Bd3 d6 ← solid structure. Play ...Be6 ...Qc7 ...O-O-O (kingside safer here) Plan: ...Be6 ...Nd7 ...Nc5 targeting the c3 pawn chain. Bishop pair shines in open positions.
Why Nxd4! first? After 7.f3, White threatens 8.g4-g5 launching the full kingside attack. By playing 7...Nxd4! Austin exchanges off the central knight BEFORE White can castle long and begin the attack. The resulting position after bxc3 is structurally sound for Black: bishop pair, c3-pawn weakness, and a playable endgame.

Branch C — 7.f3 Nxd4 variation: if White plays 8.Qxd4 (not 8.Bxd4)

7.f3 Nxd4 8.Qxd4 e5 ← attack the queen, gain space 9.Qd2 Bxc3! ← still take the c3 knight — same bishop pair idea 10.Qxc3 d6 ← solid. ...Be6 ...Nd7 ...O-O plan.

Vadim Drill B — 20 minutes

  • Tabiya (FEN above). Vadim plays 6.Be3. Austin plays 6...Bb4! without pause. Goal: under 3 seconds.
  • Branch A: Vadim plays 7.Ndb5 d6 8.a3. Austin plays Bxc3+! 9.Nxc3 d5! — verbalize: "I'm breaking in the center while his knight structure is weakened."
  • Branch B: Vadim plays 7.f3. Austin plays 7...Nxd4! 8.Bxd4 Bxc3+ 9.bxc3 e5. Play this out 5 moves each to feel the bishop pair structure.
  • Test: Vadim alternates 6.Ndb5 and 6.Be3 randomly. Austin must play d6! or Bb4! without pausing to identify which is which. 10 repetitions.

Bonus — English Attack Full Race (when Austin plays ...a6 instead of ...Bb4)

Against Fernando (the primary English Attack user), Austin may choose the practical ...a6 ...b5 plan instead of the theoretical Bb4! move. This plan must be complete by move 12 or Austin loses the race.

6.Be3 a6 ← preparing ...b5 without allowing Ndb5 7.f3 b5 ← queenside expansion starts NOW, not later 8.Qd2 Bb7 9.O-O-O Rc8 ← rook on c-file BEFORE White plays g4 10.g4 Nd4! ← attack the Qd2, complicate before White's g5 The RACE: Austin's ...b4 must land before White's g5. If Rc8 is not played by move 12, Austin is too slow.
Race timing rule: If you reach move 12 without having played ...Rc8, you have already lost the race. Fernando's kingside attack (g4-g5-h4-h5) is faster than Austin's queenside if the rook is not active. This is non-negotiable.

SESSION 2 Batrikov Variation (6.g4) + Maróczy Bind (5.c4)

Why this session exists: Three NAYCC opponents can walk Austin into positions he has never studied: Fernando (2085) may play 6.g4 Batrikov, Pranav (1922) uses the Maróczy Bind as his main Taimanov weapon (observed WY R10), and Inti (2011) plays aggressive Sicilian White setups. Austin has ZERO rapid games from the Batrikov position and zero Maróczy prep confirmed. A 5-minute clock burn on move 6 is irreversible in G/90+30. Both positions have a clear, learnable plan — this session makes those plans automatic.

Part A — Batrikov Variation (6.g4): Zero Master Games, Still Dangerous

About the name: This line is sometimes called the "Keres Attack" in the Scheveningen (with ...d6), but in the Taimanov (with ...Nc6 already played), 6.g4 is more accurately the Batrikov Variation. It has zero games in the master database — White is playing a pure gambling attack. If Austin knows the plan, White's g4 gains nothing and the structural concession (weakened f5, h4 squares) benefits Black long-term.
RULE: 6.g4 → play 6...d6! (same d6 rule!) Do NOT play 6...h6.
The d6 rule applies regardless of White's 6th move. After d6, the knight has multiple good squares (Nd7, Nh5) and Austin's queenside plan begins normally.

Why NOT 6...h6?

After 6...h6, White simply plays 7.h4 and the h-file becomes contested on White's terms. White welcomes the challenge. 6...d6! is better because it doesn't feed White's attack — it instead begins Austin's queenside counter.

Main line after 6.g4 d6!

6.g4 d6! ← INSTANT. Same d6 rule. 7.g5 Nd7 ← knight retreats safely. Nh5 is also fine. 8.Be3 a6 9.Na3 b5! ← queenside counter immediately. Na3 stuck on rim again. 10.Be2 Bb7 11.O-O Be7 ← complete development, castle next move 12.f4 O-O ← castle KINGSIDE despite g5 pawn threat — it's safe Plan: ...Nc5 → ...Nd4 to attack Qd2 or b2. White's g5 pawn is a long-term weakness, not a strength.

Alternative: 7...Nh5!? (aggressive counter)

7.g5 Nh5!? ← more dynamic; knight goes to a strong square 8.Be3 Be7 ← solid development 9.Qd2 O-O ← king safe, knight on h5 causes g5 to feel overextended Plan: ...Nf4! jumping to f4 when Be3 is on that square — wins the bishop pair with tempo.
The strategic truth about 6.g4: White has moved a kingside pawn twice (g2-g4-g5) before developing pieces. Austin now has two free moves to develop. The g5 pawn becomes a target after ...h6 (later, after development). The f5 square — created by White's g4 — is a permanent outpost for Black's pieces. This line is comfortable for Black with correct play.

Part B — Maróczy Bind (5.c4): The 6-Move Structural Plan

The Maróczy Bind arises when White plays 5.c4 after 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6. (Note: Pranav reaches it via the Taimanov move order — Austin's 5...Nf6 invites 5.c4 specifically because Nc6 is already on the board.)

What the Maróczy Bind does

e4 + c4 pawn chaind5 square is permanently controlled — no ...d5 breakAccept it: build with d6 instead
Nd5 threatWhite's best piece is a knight on d5 Austin can't challengePre-empt with ...Nxd5 exd5 OR block with ...e5 (carefully)
Closed queenside...b5 requires ...a6 preparation firstAlways ...a6 before ...b5
THE 6-MOVE PLAN (memorize this sequence):
d6 → g6 → Bg7 → O-O → a6 → b5
In that exact order. This is Austin's Maróczy structure. Do NOT deviate.

Full drill line

1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.c4 d6! ← NOT e5, NOT b6, NOT e6. Solid central anchor. 6.Nc3 g6 ← fianchetto; the Bg7 will pressure the e4 pawn long-term 7.f3 Bg7 8.Be3 O-O ← castle BEFORE expanding 9.Be2 a6 ← prepares ...b5, stops Na4/Na5 ideas 10.O-O Rb8 ← rook on b-file, ready to support ...b5 push 11.Qd2 b5! ← the break. Challenge the c4 pawn. 12.cxb5 axb5 ← open the a-file for ...Ra8 pressure, or recapture with Nd4 after Nb3 Plan: ...Bb7 → ...Nd4 → ...Nc2 or ...Nxe2+ fork ideas on the queenside

The Nd5 problem — White's main weapon

In the Maróczy, White always wants to play Nd5. When White plays Nd5, Austin should NOT ignore it or play around it. The correct response:

After White plays Nd5 (usually around move 12–15): ... Nxd5! ← take it immediately. Don't leave the knight there. cxd5 or exd5 ← recapture. Now the c-file is open for Black's rooks. ... Nc5 or Nd7 ← regroup the knight to a useful square The resulting IQP/isolated pawn structure is familiar to Austin from Nimzo-Indian experience.
Do NOT play ...e5 as first move in the Maróczy. After 5.c4 e5?, White plays 6.Ndb5! and now Nd6+ is a real threat. The d6 square becomes permanently weak. Then 7.Nd5! lands with devastating effect. The e5 move looks natural but is tactically refuted by Nd5.
Do NOT play ...b5 before ...a6. After 5.c4 and Austin plays ...b5 without ...a6 preparation, White plays a4! and the b5 pawn is hanging. Austin loses a pawn with no compensation.
Why the fianchetto (g6/Bg7)? The Bg7 controls the long diagonal and permanently challenges the e4 pawn. White cannot easily play d4 (it's already played). White cannot easily play f4 without weakening g4. The Bg7 is Austin's long-term pressure piece — once it's on g7, it works by itself.

Vadim Drill B — 25 minutes

  • Tabiya FEN (Session 1). Vadim plays 5.c4. Austin plays the 6-move plan from memory: d6, g6, Bg7, O-O, a6, b5. Verbalize each move's purpose as you play it.
  • Vadim plays Nd5 on move 12. Austin plays Nxd5 cxd5 immediately without pausing. Then continues plan with ...Nc5 or ...Nd7 regrouping.
  • Play a full rapid game from the Maróczy position (Austin Black, Vadim White). Vadim plays normal Maróczy setups. Goal: reach move 20 with ...b5 played and rooks active.
  • Vadim tests: "What do you do if I play a4 before you've played ...a6?" Austin answers: "b4!" (closing the queenside) — ...b4 after a4 is the correct counter since a4 cedes the b4 square.

SESSION 3 Bxh7+ Greek Gift — Recognition Before Calculation

Why this session exists (engine data): In 32 personal study games, Austin missed the Bxh7+ sacrifice in exactly 4 positions, each time dropping from a winning position to equality. The losses represent approximately 600–1000 CPL each. This is NOT a calculation failure — Austin can calculate Bxh7+ correctly once he starts. The problem is that Austin is not noticing the pattern is available before choosing his move. A GM doesn't calculate every possibility from scratch — they first recognize the pattern, then calculate. This session builds the recognition reflex.

The Greek Gift Sacrifice — Complete Pattern

Required conditions (all four must be present)

ConditionCheck forCommon position
1. Bishop on the diagonalWhite's bishop on d3 (or c2, e4) with clear line to h7Exchange CK: Bd3 after 4.Bd3
2. Knight reachable to g5White's knight on f3 (or e5) can jump to g5 next moveNf3 is standard in Exchange CK
3. Queen can reach h5White's queen is NOT blocked from h5 (by own pieces)Qe2 or Qd1 → h5 in 1–2 moves
4. Black's king exposedKing castled g8, pawn on h7 (NOT h6 — h6 blocks the sacrifice!)Standard short castle with h7 intact
THE 3-QUESTION CHECKLIST — run this BEFORE every move when ahead by +2 or more:
Q1: Is my bishop pointing at h7 with a clear diagonal?
Q2: Can my knight reach g5 in one move?
Q3: Can my queen reach h5 or d3-h7 diagonal after Ng5+?
If YES to all three → STOP and calculate Bxh7+ before playing anything else.

The standard Bxh7+ combination — full calculation

Position: Austin has Bd3, Nf3, Queen available. Black king on g8, pawn on h7. Bxh7+! ← sacrifice the bishop. White gives up 3 points to win by force. Kxh7 ← Black must take. If Kh8, Ng5 with Qh5 threat anyway — still winning. Ng5+ ← NOW the knight comes in with check. This is the point of Bxh7+. Continuation A — Kg8: Qh5! ← queen goes to h5. Two threats: Qxh7# and Qxf7# ...if h6: Nxf7! ← fork Qd8 and Rh8. Decisive. ...if Nxg5: Qxf7+ Kh8 Qh5# ← or similar forced mate Continuation B — Kg6: Qg4! (or h4-h5) ← trap the king. Threatens f4-f5# mate net. h5: Qf3! ← or Qh3, threatening Qh4-f6 with inevitable mate Continuation C — Kh6: Nxe6+! ← don't go back. Fork the king and queen. Kxg5: Qd2+ Kh4 g3# ← or other forced mate lines

Exchange CK — the primary Bxh7+ factory

Austin's Exchange Caro-Kann setup (1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5 3.exd5 cxd5 4.Bd3 Nc6 5.c3 Nf6 6.Bf4) places the bishop on d3 by move 4. This is Austin's standard opening as White — meaning Bxh7+ is always a possibility against anyone who castles kingside.

Standard Exchange CK position where Bxh7+ will arise: 1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5 3.exd5 cxd5 4.Bd3 Nc6 5.c3 Nf6 6.Bf4 Bg4 7.Qb3 Qd7 8.Nd2 e6 9.Ngf3 Bd6 10.h3 Bh5 Position: White has Bd3 pointing at h7, Nf3, and Queen on b3 (not ideal for h5 but Qe2 coming).
