Part 1 — Rematch Fix: What Went Wrong at NAYCC R4

The mistake: After 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.e5 c5 4.c3 Nc6 5.Nf3 Nge7, Austin played 6.dxc5?! — taking the gambit pawn immediately. This gave Justin free piece development: 6…Ng6 7.Be3 Ngxe5! — his knight sat powerfully on e5 and Austin never recovered central control. The game became a passed-pawn race where Justin's d-pawn (d4-d3) decided it.

The exact move-order that failed

Austin: 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.e5 c5 4.c3 Nc6 5.Nf3 Nge7 6.dxc5?! Ng6 7.Be3 Ngxe5! — Justin gets active piece immediately. The c5 pawn was not worth the activity given to Black.

Fix A — Switch to Exchange French (recommended)

The cleanest fix is to change opening entirely: play 3.exd5 exd5 (Exchange French, C01). Justin's record in this variation is 0W-1D-1L (lost to Plotkin V 2260, drew with Wu 1906). The Exchange French immediately changes the character of the game — symmetrical pawn structure, open e-file, no pawn chain tension. Justin cannot use his NAYCC Advanced French preparation.
Austin: 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.exd5! exd5 4.Nf3 Nf6 5.Bd3 Bd6 6.O-O O-O 7.Bg5! — pin the f6 knight; target d5 pawn. Austin develops with pressure from move 1.

If Austin prefers the Advanced French: after 5.Nf3 Nge7, play 6.a3! (same system as Fernando prep) — NOT 6.dxc5. The 6.a3 system prepares b4 and avoids handing Justin piece activity. Justin has never faced 6.a3 in this dataset.

Part 3 — Austin as Black vs Justin's 1.d4

Core fact: Justin plays 1.d4 with the QGD/Semi-Slav setup — d4-c4-Nf3-Nc3-Bd3/Be2. Austin plays Nimzo-Indian (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Bb4!) — his main Black weapon. Justin plays Nc3 (Ragozin, Semi-Slav, QGD), so the Nimzo arises naturally. Austin's Bxc3+ with …b6/…Ba6 structure gives concrete imbalances that Justin's positional style finds difficult.

Justin's 1.d4 setup (confirmed OTB)

  • Always 1.d4 — no exceptions in 10+ White games
  • Typical: d4-c4-Nf3-Nc3 → Bd3 (QGD) or Be2 (Rubinstein-style)
  • Against KID: 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6 3.Nc3 Bg7 4.e4 (classical KID)

Austin's weapon: Nimzo-Indian Bxc3+ with …b6/…Ba6

Justin: 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Bb4! (Nimzo) 4.e3 O-O 5.Bd3 c5! 6.Nf3 d5 7.O-O Bxc3! 8.bxc3 dxc4! 9.Bxc4 Qc7 10.Qe2 b6! — Austin's signature Nimzo: Bxc3+ then ...b6/...Ba6 to exploit doubled c-pawns. IQP structure where Justin's queenside majority is neutralized.
Justin: 4.e3 O-O 5.Bd3 c5 6.Nf3 d5 7.O-O Bxc3! 8.bxc3 dxc4! 9.Bxc4 Qc7 10.Qe2 b6! 11.e4 cxd4 12.cxd4 Ba6! — Ba6 pins the d3 bishop (or targets the c4 bishop); White's doubled c-pawns are chronic weaknesses.

After 12…Ba6: Austin's bishop on a6 stares at Justin's queenside pawns. White's isolated d-pawn and doubled c-pawns give Austin long-term pressure in the endgame. Justin's strength is in closed positions; this IQP structure forces him to be active tactically — his weakness.

Justin: 4.Qc2 O-O 5.a3 Bxc3+! 6.Qxc3 b6 7.Bg5 Bb7 8.e3 d6! 9.Bd3 Nbd7 10.Ne2 c5! — Black equalizes with the …b6/…Bb7/…c5 queenside majority. Justin gets no bishops pair advantage since Austin has traded proactively.

Key: After 4.Qc2, Austin should still play Bxc3+ on move 5 (after a3 forces it), not retreat. The Qc2 system avoids doubled pawns but Black gets a solid position with ...d6 /...c5 queenside play. Austin's tactical style suits the resulting asymmetric middlegame.

The Sämisch (4.f3) is the most aggressive anti-Nimzo. Justin hasn't played it in the dataset, but if he does:

Justin: 4.f3 d5 5.a3 Bxc3+! 6.bxc3 c5 7.cxd5 Nxd5! 8.dxc5 Qa5 9.e4 Nf4! — Nf4 targets d3 and creates immediate tactical complications. Austin's tactical style thrives in Sämisch positions.
