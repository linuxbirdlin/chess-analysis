Part 1 — Austin as White: Italian Classical — Central Squeeze vs Solid 1…e5

The core challenge: Inti plays 1…e5 exclusively — Austin's normal Rossolimo, Exchange Caro-Kann, and Advanced French preparation does not apply. Austin must face an Italian/Ruy Lopez player. The weapon: Italian Classical with the d4 central break (7.Re1 → h3 → Nbd2-Nf1-Ng3 → d4!). Inti's Italian as Black is tactically active — but his accuracy collapses when positionally squeezed past move 20 against stronger players (Agua R1: CM played d4 at move 18, gained space, Inti never recovered). Austin's plan: contain his pieces, build central pressure, deliver the d4 break with maximum force.

Italian Classical — Austin's main line vs 3…Bc5

Austin: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.c3 Nf6 5.d3 d6 6.O-O O-O 7.Re1 a6 8.h3! — Critical prophylaxis. Prevents …Bg4 pin against the f3-knight — Inti used this in Copa 2024 R1 and Querétaro R1 to generate piece activity. Play h3 before Nbd2 every time.
Austin: 8.h3 Re8 9.Nbd2 Nd7 10.Nf1 Nf8 11.Ng3 Ne6 12.d4! exd4 13.cxd4 Bb6 14.d5 Nc5 15.Bc2 — The d4-d5 space gain is Austin's goal. Black's knights are restricted, the queenside is locked, and Austin builds b4-b5 or Nh5-Nf5 kingside pressure. Inti had this done to him at Agua R1 — his accuracy fell immediately.

After 15.Bc2: plan b4 (pushing the c5-knight away), then a4-a5 or Nh5-Nf5. The Ng3-Nh5-Nf5 maneuver is the classic Italian squeeze — Inti's Nd7-Nf8-Ne6 retreat pattern shows he knows the defensive setup, so Austin must deny ...Nf4 at all times (play g3 if the f4 square opens).

Key rule: If Inti plays …Nd4 at any point, consider exchanging on d4 (cxd4 or Nxd4) and recapturing with the c-pawn or the other knight — do not allow the centralized knight to sit uncontested.

Austin: 8.h3 Ba7 9.Nbd2 d5 10.exd5! Nxd5 11.Nxe5! Nxe5 12.Rxe5 Nf6 13.d4 — Ba7 often precedes the …d5 central break. After 10.exd5 Nxd5 11.Nxe5!, White wins the e5 pawn cleanly: if 11…Nxe5 12.Rxe5, White is up a pawn with the centralized rook. If Black avoids …d5 and plays …h6 or …Re8 instead, Austin simply continues Nf1-Ng3-d4 as in Branch A — Ba7 does not change White's strategy.

After 13.d4: Austin has gained central space and a pawn. If Black plays 13…Bg4 (pinning the f3-knight), Austin answers 14.h3 (already played) keeping the structure clean. Continue with Bg5 pressure and the long-term squeeze. Note: Ba7 cannot allow 9…Ne5 directly — the e5 pawn is still there. The bishop retreat only prepares the …d5 break or passive development; Austin continues normally either way.

Austin: 4.c3 Nf6 5.d3 d5!? 6.exd5! Nxd5 7.O-O O-O 8.Re1 Bg4 9.h3! Bh5 10.Nbd2 Nf4 11.Nxe5 Nxe2+ 12.Rxe2 — After 6.exd5 Nxd5, do NOT play 7.Nxe5?! (7…Nxe5 8.Rxe5?? loses to 8…Nxf2!). Play 7.O-O and keep central tension. After 9.h3!, the Bh5 bishop is stuck outside Black's queenside — a permanent liability. The Nf4 attempt is met with Nxe5 and the bishop remains misplaced.

After 12.Rxe2: Austin has the bishop pair, the rook is centralized, and Black's pieces lack coordination. Continue with d4, opening the center. Inti has never been documented playing this line — it would be unfamiliar territory for him at this stage.

Austin: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5! a6 4.Ba4 Nf6 5.O-O Bc5 6.c3 b5 7.Bc2 d6 8.d4! Bg4 9.Be3 — The Lopez Chigorin-type. Inti plays Bc5-b5 as Black in the Lopez — he knows this structure from the Black side (C77/C78). Tradeoff: Inti has documented López theory — he won Querétaro R3 as Black in C69 Exchange. Use this only if Austin is more comfortable here than Italian.

After 9.Be3: plan Nbd2-Nf1-Ng3 (same Italian knight maneuver) or d5 to gain space. Inti's worst documented Lopez game (Agua R1, C77) showed accuracy dropping to 91.1% vs CM — Austin should aim for the same positional squeeze.

Part 2 — Austin as Black: Taimanov (5…a6) — Castle Kingside, Queenside Squeeze

The core insight: Inti's B43 Sicilian Kan f4 system is his biggest White vulnerability in the entire database — 14 blunders, 7 mistakes in a single game (Querétaro R6). Austin's Taimanov (5…Nc6 5…a6) enters this exact structure. The single most important rule: castle KINGSIDE. De Alba's 11…O-O-O?? handed Inti +3.0 instantly — Inti had the a-file attack pre-prepared. With Austin castled kingside, Inti's f4-e5 push loses its target, and Austin's …b5-b4 queenside break dismantles the position.

Taimanov 5…a6 — Austin's defense vs Inti's Open Sicilian

Inti: 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 e6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nc6 5.Nc3 a6 — Taimanov Sicilian. After 5.Nc3 a6, Austin reaches the Taimanov structure. Inti's primary response based on Querétaro R6 is 6.Bd3 building toward f4. The critical rule: whatever happens next, castle KINGSIDE.
Inti: 5.Nc3 a6 6.Bd3 Nf6 7.O-O Be7 8.Kh1 O-O! — KINGSIDE only. Never O-O-O here. Querétaro R6 proof: De Alba played O-O-O and handed Inti +3.0 instantly. With O-O, Inti's f4-f5 push has no target and Austin begins queenside play. 9.f4 d6 10.Nxc6 bxc6 11…b5! — The …b5 push begins the queenside squeeze. Inti has no prepared plan once the kingside attack loses its target. In Querétaro R6, once Black got …b4/…bxc3, Inti's position collapsed in 14 blunders.

After 11…b5: plan …Bb7, …Nd7, …Nc5 with queenside pressure. If Inti plays f5, answer …e5 closing the kingside — Inti has no documented plan against a closed kingside. The critical queenside break …b4 should come when Nc3 is on the board: it disrupts coordination and creates passed pawns (exactly the pattern that ended Querétaro R6).

Inti: 6.Be3 Qc7 7.Bd3 Nf6 8.O-O Be7 9.f4 d6 10.Kh1 O-O! 11.Qe1 Nxd4 12.Bxd4 b5 13…b4! — The English Attack is tamer than 6.Bd3 because Be3 commits the bishop early. After …Nxd4/Bxd4, Austin eliminates the critical d4-knight and launches …b5-b4 immediately. The b4-push attacks Nc3 and begins active queenside counterplay — the exact structure Inti mishandled in Querétaro R6.

After 13…b4: if 14.Nd1 Bb7 15.Ne3 d5! — central break is strong. If 14.Na4 Bb7 15.c3 bxc3 16.bxc3 c5 — Austin's queenside initiative compensates for the kingside attack. Inti's Copa 2024 pattern (Sicilian B89, B90) shows he understands English Attack setups — expect him to play f4-f5 quickly. Austin must generate queenside play before move 20.

Inti: 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 e6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nc6 5.Nb5 a6 6.Nd6+ Bxd6 7.Qxd6 Qf6! — Austin's Taimanov starts 2…e6 (not 2…Nc6). If Inti plays 5.Nb5 (instead of the normal 5.Nc3), 6.Nd6+ gives check to the king on e8 and forks the c8 bishop — same trick whether Black's pawn is on e5 or e6. 7…Qf6 is the theoretical equalizer: attacks the d6 queen, threatens …Qxb2 (awkward), and forces 8.Qd2 Qg6 → …Nf6 → …d5 with active counterplay. Querétaro R2 shows Inti scored 98.9% in a similar Nd6+ position — his opponent didn't know the response. Austin knowing Qf6! removes this edge.

After 8.Qd2 Qg6: plan 9…Nf6 10…d5 central break with full equality. If Inti plays 5.Nc3 a6 (the standard Taimanov path), proceed to Branch A (6.Bd3). 5.Nb5 is a side-deviation — Austin needs Qf6! ready but this line is far less likely than 5.Nc3.

Inti: 5.Nc3 a6 6.Bc4 Nf6 7.O-O Be7 8.Bb3 d6 9.f4 O-O! 10.f5 e5 — Inti's Bc4 puts the bishop on an attacking diagonal but overextends early. After O-O (kingside again!), if Inti plays f5 immediately, Austin answers …e5 to close the kingside. This cuts off the f5-pawn's support and leaves Inti with a pawn weakness. Plan: …Nd4/…Nc5 central outpost, then …b5-b4 queenside break as usual.

Inti's B35 modern Bc4 system (Querétaro R2, 98.9% win vs 1519) was against a much lower-rated opponent. Against Austin's prepared Taimanov, Bc4 is less dangerous than Bd3 because it doesn't immediately threaten f4-f5 with piece coordination. The …e5 close + …Nd4 outpost neutralizes it.
