Part A — Austin as White: Tarrasch (3.Nd2)

NEVER play Exchange French (3.exd5). Leo won a 134-move endgame in Exchange French structure as Black (NA U20 R8 vs Cruz Vargas 1648). This is his comfort zone.
NEVER play Advance French (3.e5). Not Austin's 2026 system — abandoned after 2025. Austin's weapon is Tarrasch (3.Nd2) exclusively.

Main Line: Tarrasch Chistyakov (Leo's best-prepared response)

1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.Nd2! c5 4.exd5 Qxd5 5.Ngf3! cxd4 6.Bc4 Qd6 7.0-0 Nf6 8.Nb3 — 5.Ngf3! (Lichess masters: 99% of games — NEVER 5.Ndf3). After 5...cxd4: 6.Bc4 Qd6 7.0-0 — Bc4 eyes f7. 8.Nb3 heads to c5 or d4. Leo's queen moved twice by move 5 — White has active piece play.
Critical rule: 5.Ngf3, NOT 5.Ndf3. After 3.Nd2 c5 4.exd5 Qxd5, the correct move is 5.Ngf3 (the knight from g1, not from d2). This is 99% of Lichess master games. 5...cxd4 follows virtually always (99%). Then 6.Bc4 Qd6 7.0-0 — fast development with Bc4 threat on f7.

Continuation after 8.Nb3

8.Nb3 Nc6 9.Re1 a6 10.Bd3! — Bc4 traded or retreated to Bd3 once Black plays ...Nc6. Re1 pins the e-file. Austin has active piece coordination; Leo must defend concretely. This is the type of concrete calculation problem Leo miscalculates.

Alternate Line: Tarrasch 3...Nf6

3.Nd2 Nf6 4.e5 Nfd7 5.Bd3 c5 6.c3 Nc6 7.Ngf3 Qb6 8.0-0 — Standard Tarrasch structure. White holds e5 chain; Leo's ...Qb6 pressure on d4 and b2 is answered with Re1 and Ndf3-Ng3 setup. Plan: f4-f5 kingside advance.

Part B — Austin as Black: Queen's Indian vs Leo's Catalan

Leo plays 3.Nf3 (not 3.Nc3) to avoid the Nimzo-Indian. Austin's standard Nimzo (3.Nc3 Bb4) is unavailable. Primary response: Queen's Indian (3...b6). Secondary: Bogo-Indian (3...Bb4+) or Open Catalan (3...d5 dxc4).

Primary: Queen's Indian — 3...b6

Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 b6! 4.g3 Bb7 5.Bg2 Be7 6.0-0 0-0 7...Ne4! — Ne4 is the engine of the Queen's Indian system. Forces Leo to resolve central tension. After Nxe4 dxe4, Austin has active bishop on b7. This mirrors Austin's Nimzo ...Ne4 habits.
Queen's Indian key ideas: ...Ne4, ...Ba6, ...c5, ...d5. Same strategic patterns Austin uses in his Nimzo-Indian — the ...Ne4 jump, ...Ba6 pressure on c4, ...c5 IQP structure. Familiar territory.

Backup: Bogo-Indian — 3...Bb4+

Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 Bb4+ 4.Bd2 Bxd2+! 5.Qxd2 — Bxd2+ gives Leo the bishop pair but costs a tempo. Austin plays ...c5 or ...b6 for active, unbalanced play. Slightly Nimzo-flavored without structural commitment.

Backup: Open Catalan — 3...d5 dxc4

Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 d5 4.g3 dxc4! 5.Bg2 — Grabbing the pawn creates early imbalance. Leo's Bg2 points at nothing. Austin defends with ...a6, ...b5, ...Bb7.
AVOID passive QGD (3...d5 4.g3 Be7 without pawn grab). Long technical draws where Leo's patience matters. Austin needs unbalanced positions, not symmetrical endgames.

Part C — Leo's Weakness Patterns: Where He Collapses

Weakness 1: Knight Coordination Collapse

NA U20 R4 vs Fogo Esquivel (2190) — Nb4 (CPL 456) and Nc6 (CPL 440) back-to-back from −0.38. Leo had a near-equal position and played two consecutive massive errors involving knight repositioning. The game was effectively over after these two moves. Pattern: when Leo must reposition a knight under middlegame pressure, he chooses the aggressive-looking square (b4, c6) that loses by force. Austin should deliberately create positions where Leo's knight needs to find a precise square.

Weakness 2: Rook to Wrong File

NA U20 R4 vs Fogo — Rf1 (best Rg1) CPL 1000 after the knight collapse. After the knight errors, Leo also misplaced his rook. When concrete calculation is required for rook placement, Leo defaults to the structural-looking square (f1) instead of the active one (g1). Austin should force positions where precise rook placement is the difference between winning and losing.

Weakness 3: Missed Winning Pawn Capture

Am Cont R8 vs Monroy (2062) — b5 (best cxd4) CPL 509 from +5.34 → drew. With a completely winning position (+5.34), Leo played a structural pawn move (b5) instead of the concrete winning pawn capture (cxd4). He drew the game. Pattern: when a pawn move is critical, Leo plays a piece/structural move instead — calculate the pawn capture and trust it.

Weakness 4: King Endgame Navigation

NA U20 R8 vs Cruz Vargas — Kh7 (best Kh5) CPL 363 from +3.63 → draw. In a winning K+P endgame, Leo moved his king to the wrong square. He still won eventually, but the error shows his king navigation is unreliable even against much weaker opponents. Force king-and-pawn endings when ahead.

Leo's Endgame Grinding Strength (know this, don't fall into it)

Leo has won/drawn in 134, 149, 152-move endgames. His patience in technical endgames is genuine. Austin must not let the game reach a purely technical ending before move 40. Create decisive middlegame moments. If the game simplifies too early (before a concrete advantage is established), Leo's grinding ability becomes a real factor.
